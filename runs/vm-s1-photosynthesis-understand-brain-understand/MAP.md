## Concepts

### Photosynthesis in plant physiology and the living world · process

Basis: book. Plant physiology connects molecular and cellular events with whole-plant function and environmental conditions, complementing the organismic perspective associated with ecology. Photosynthesis is a physico-chemical process using light energy to synthesise organic food; the chapter contrasts autotrophic plants with dependent heterotrophs and emphasises food supply and atmospheric oxygen, with CO₂ entering leaves through stomata.

### Experimental requirements: chlorophyll, light and carbon dioxide · cause_effect

Basis: book. Starch formation in illuminated green regions, compared with non-green or black-paper-covered regions, provides evidence for chlorophyll and light requirements. KOH removes CO₂ from the enclosed portion of a leaf; its failure to accumulate starch while the air-exposed portion does so supports the requirement for CO₂ through a controlled comparison.

### Priestley and Ingenhousz: restoration of air and oxygen release · timeline

Basis: book. Priestley's 1770 bell-jar comparisons suggested that plants restore what burning candles and breathing animals remove from air; the figure has two separate before–after sequences, with and without a plant, and oxygen was identified by Priestley in 1774. Ingenhousz's light–dark comparisons and oxygen bubbles around illuminated green aquatic-plant parts established the roles of sunlight and green tissues; the rekindling question tests how to examine restored air without opening the setup.

### Sachs and Engelmann: carbohydrate production and action spectrum · process

Basis: book. Around 1854, Sachs linked green chloroplast-containing parts with glucose production and storage as starch. Engelmann illuminated Cladophora with prism-separated light and used aerobic-bacterial accumulation to detect oxygen evolution, obtaining an early action spectrum with strong blue and red responses; together such work established light-driven carbohydrate formation from CO₂ and water.

### Hydrogen donors, oxygen source and overall equations · facts_numbers

Basis: book. The empirical CO₂ + H₂O → [CH₂O] + O₂ equation is expanded by van Niel's donor generalisation, 2H₂A + CO₂ → 2A + CH₂O + H₂O: water is the donor in green plants, whereas H₂S-using bacteria produce sulphur or sulphate rather than oxygen. The overall plant equation is 6CO₂ + 12H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6H₂O + 6O₂ in light, describing many reactions rather than one; retaining substrate and product water makes the water-derived oxygen accounting explicit.

### Chloroplast location, structure and division of labour · structure

Basis: book. Photosynthesis occurs in leaves and other green parts; mesophyll chloroplasts position themselves along cell walls to obtain suitable incident light, and the orientation question asks students to reason about their flat surfaces and light direction. Identify the outer and inner envelope membranes, grana, connecting stromal lamellae, stroma, ribosomes, starch granules and lipid droplets: membranes carry light trapping and ATP/NADPH formation, whereas stromal enzyme reactions synthesise sugars, so 'dark reaction' means indirect rather than absent light dependence.

### Leaf pigments and their chromatographic colours · classification

Basis: book. Paper chromatography reveals chlorophyll a, bright or blue-green; chlorophyll b, yellow-green; xanthophylls, yellow; and carotenoids, yellow to yellow-orange, showing that leaf colour comes from a pigment mixture. Pigments absorb particular wavelengths; the abundance, dark-leaf yellowing and shade-versus-sun questions should be distinguished from these directly stated observations, and explanations of pigment stability or shade acclimation require an outside basis.

### Absorption spectra, action spectra and accessory-pigment functions · comparison

Basis: book and supplied figure descriptions. Absorption spectra plot pigment absorption against wavelength, whereas the action spectrum plots photosynthetic rate measured by O₂ release: chlorophyll a has prominent blue and red peaks, chlorophyll b has shifted peaks, and carotenoids absorb mainly blue light. The spectra correspond but do not overlap completely: chlorophyll a is the chief pigment, while accessory pigments transfer absorbed energy to it, widen usable wavelengths and protect it from photo-oxidation; intermediate-wavelength photosynthesis is not zero, and the superimposed curves do not share a numerical rate–absorption scale.

### Light-harvesting complexes and reaction centres · structure

Basis: book. The photochemical phase includes absorption, water splitting, oxygen release and ATP/NADPH formation; hundreds of protein-bound antenna pigments funnel photon energy to the reaction-centre chlorophyll a, which transfers an excited electron to a primary acceptor. PS I contains P700 and PS II contains P680, named for absorption maxima of 700 and 680 nm; photosystem numbering follows discovery, not functional order.

### Non-cyclic electron transport and the Z scheme · process

Basis: book. Light excites P680 electrons to an acceptor; carriers pass them downhill to PS I, where light excites electrons again and a second acceptor pathway ultimately reduces NADP⁺ to NADPH. Electrons are transferred rather than used up, and the two light-driven rises and intervening descents produce the Z-shaped arrangement on a redox-potential scale; non-cyclic flow operates PS II then PS I and supports ATP and NADPH formation.

### Water splitting supplies replacement electrons · process

Basis: book. Electrons lost from PS II are replaced by its associated water-splitting complex, while PS II supplies replacement electrons to PS I through the transport chain. The reaction 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + O₂ + 4e⁻ releases protons on the lumen-facing side of the thylakoid membrane and establishes water as the source of evolved oxygen.

### Cyclic versus non-cyclic photophosphorylation · comparison

Basis: book. Photophosphorylation forms ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate using light; in cyclic flow, excited PS I electrons return through carriers to P700 instead of reducing NADP⁺, producing ATP without NADPH. Contrast this with the PS II-to-PS I non-cyclic pathway; the book identifies stromal lamellae lacking PS II and NADP reductase as a possible cyclic-flow location and also associates cyclic flow with excitation wavelengths beyond 680 nm.

### Chemiosmosis: proton gradient and ATP synthase · cause_effect

Basis: book. Water oxidation adds lumen protons, hydrogen-carrier transport moves protons from stroma to lumen, and stromal NADP⁺ reduction removes stromal protons, producing high lumen H⁺, lower lumen pH and an electrochemical gradient. ### Calvin's investigation and indirect light dependence of biosynthesis · process

Basis: book. Light-reaction oxygen diffuses out, while ATP and NADPH drive stromal sugar synthesis; biosynthesis briefly continues after light removal and then stops, demonstrating indirect light dependence rather than a requirement for darkness. Calvin, born in Minnesota in April 1911, earned his chemistry Ph.D. there and worked at Berkeley; his post-war work with Bassham using ¹⁴CO₂ identified three-carbon PGA and mapped carbon assimilation, earning the 1961 Nobel Prize, while his electron-transfer ideas and photosynthetic principles connect to solar-energy and renewable-resource research.

### Calvin-cycle energy budget and supplementary cyclic ATP · facts_numbers

Basis: book. Each CO₂ fixed requires three ATP and two NADPH; six turns fix six CO₂ to produce the glucose-equivalent output, consuming eighteen ATP and twelve NADPH and returning eighteen ADP and twelve oxidised NADP carriers. ### C₄ adaptations and Kranz anatomy · structure

### RuBisCO competition, photorespiration and C₄ productivity · cause_effect

Basis: book. RuBisCO, described as the most abundant enzyme, competitively binds CO₂ or O₂; normal RuBP carboxylation yields two PGA molecules, whereas oxygenation yields one phosphoglycerate and a two-carbon phosphoglycolate, reducing carbon fixation. Photorespiration consumes ATP and releases CO₂ without producing sugars, ATP or NADPH; C₄-acid decarboxylation raises bundle sheath CO₂ around RuBisCO, minimises oxygenase activity and explains the chapter's absence of C₄ photorespiration and higher productivity.

### Internal and external factors and Blackman's law · cause_effect

Basis: book. Internal controls include leaf number, size, age and orientation, mesophyll and chloroplast characteristics, internal CO₂ and chlorophyll, linked to genetic predisposition and growth; external controls include light, temperature, CO₂ and water. Blackman's 1905 law identifies the factor nearest its minimum as the one whose change directly changes rate, explaining why optimal light and CO₂ cannot overcome very low temperature.

### Light response, saturation and graph interpretation · cause_effect

Basis: book and supplied figure description. Distinguish light quality, intensity and duration: fixation rises approximately linearly at low intensity, bends as other factors limit it, and reaches a plateau; the book states saturation around ten per cent of full sunlight and warns that sufficiently excessive light breaks down chlorophyll and decreases photosynthesis. ### CO₂ concentration and greenhouse enrichment · comparison

Basis: book. The text gives atmospheric CO₂ as 0.03–0.04%, describes stimulation up to 0.05% with possible long-term damage beyond it, and identifies CO₂ as a major limiting factor; these are the book's values rather than a claim about present measurements. Neither C₃ nor C₄ plants responds to added CO₂ under low light, whereas under high light C₄ saturation occurs near 360 µL L⁻¹ and C₃ saturation beyond 450 µL L⁻¹, explaining CO₂-enriched greenhouse production of tomatoes and bell pepper.

### Temperature, habitat and indirect water-stress effects · cause_effect

Basis: book. Enzymatic carbon reactions are strongly temperature-controlled, while light reactions are less temperature-sensitive; C₄ plants have higher temperature optima than C₃ plants, and tropical plants higher optima than temperate-adapted plants. Water affects photosynthesis chiefly through plant condition: stress closes stomata and restricts CO₂, while wilting reduces leaf surface area and metabolic activity, rather than acting simply through a shortage of photolysis substrate.

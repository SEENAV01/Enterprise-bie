## The chapter in brief

Photosynthesis uses light energy to synthesise organic compounds and releases oxygen derived from water. Experiments establish its requirements; chloroplast membranes capture energy through photosystems, electron transport and chemiosmosis, while stromal reactions use ATP and NADPH to fix carbon. The Calvin cycle, C₄ carbon concentration, photorespiration and limiting factors together explain variation in photosynthetic efficiency and productivity.

- [p019-i1 · heading · none · exam low] 11.10 Factors affecting Photosynthesis
- [p019-i2 · teach · classification · exam high] An understanding of the factors that affect photosynthesis is necessary.   why: To understand differences in crop yield, examine the rate of photosynthesis. → That rate is influenced by internal plant features as well as external conditions. → Different amounts and arrangements of photosynthetic equipment change how effectively a leaf captures light and fixes carbon.

- [p019-i3 · teach · cause_effect · exam high · deep: misconception] The external factors would include the availability of sunlight, temperature, CO₂ concentration and water.   why: A leaf needs several environmental conditions together for photosynthesis. → One condition is usually sub-optimal and restricts the rate despite the presence of the others. → Therefore identify the bottleneck before deciding which condition to improve.

- [p019-i4 · teach · definition · exam high · deep: application] When several factors affect any [bio] chemical process, Blackman’s (1905) Law of Limiting Factors comes into effect. This states the following: If a chemical process is affected by more than one factor, then its rate will be determined by the factor which is nearest to its minimal value: it is the factor which directly affects the process if its quantity is changed.
  terms: ★ Law of Limiting Factors = कई जरूरतों में जो अभी सबसे ज्यादा रोक रही है, वही रफ्तार तय करती है; ★ minimal value = प्रक्रिया की जरूरत के हिसाब से सबसे कम अनुकूल स्तर
  why: Several factors affect one chemical process, so knowing that all matter does not identify the immediate constraint. → Blackman’s law identifies the factor nearest its minimal value as the rate-determining factor. → Changing that limiting condition provides a way to test the explanation.
  leads to: Predicts which change can improve rate under specified conditions.
  example: Apply the law to the green leaf with optimal light and CO₂ but very low temperature.
  show: change one control and trace the response
  exam: Learn Blackman, 1905, the law’s wording and its experimental application.
  needs first: rate of a reaction; multiple requirements can jointly constrain a process; sub-optimal conditions

## 11.10.2 Carbon dioxide Concentration

- [p020-i2 · heading · none · exam low] 11.10.2 Carbon dioxide Concentration
- [p020-i3 · teach · facts_numbers · exam high] Carbon dioxide is the major limiting factor for photosynthesis. The concentration of CO₂ is very low in the atmosphere (between 0.03 and 0.04 per cent). Increase in concentration upto 0.05 per cent can cause an increase in CO₂ fixation rates; beyond this the levels can become damaging over longer periods.
  terms: limiting factor; CO₂ fixation; ★ concentration = कुल हवा में CO₂ का कितना हिस्सा है
  why: Making carbohydrates requires a supply of carbon from CO₂. → The book describes atmospheric CO₂ as low and a major limiting factor. → Enrichment upto 0.05% can increase fixation, but still higher levels can damage over long periods.
  leads to: CO₂ enrichment has a useful range rather than unlimited benefit.
  example: Atmosphere supplying CO₂ to a photosynthesising leaf.
  show: Enrich the air around a leaf and show fixation increasing within the stated range.
  exam: Learn 0.03–0.04%, upto 0.05%, and the qualification that higher levels can damage over longer periods.
  needs first: carbon dioxide as a photosynthetic reactant; meaning of a limiting factor; percentage concentration
- [p020-i4 · teach · comparison · exam high · deep: subtle] The C₃ and C₄ plants respond differently to CO₂ concentrations. At low light conditions neither group responds to high CO₂ conditions. At high light intensities, both C₃ and C₄ plants show increase in the rates of photosynthesis. What is important to note is that the C₄ plants show saturation at about 360 µlL⁻¹ while C₃ responds to increased CO₂ concentration and saturation is seen only beyond 450 µlL⁻¹. Thus, current availability of CO₂ levels is limiting to the C₃ plants.
  terms: ★ saturation = अब और CO₂ देने पर rate का खास न बढ़ना; ★ µlL⁻¹ = हर litre हवा में CO₂ के microlitres; ★ C₃ and C₄ plants = carbon fixation के अलग रास्तों वाले plants, जिनके पहले stable products में 3 या 4 carbon होते हैं
  why: Ask whether extra CO₂ helps when light itself is scarce. → Neither group responds under low light; both respond under high light. → C₄ saturates near 360 µlL⁻¹ while C₃ continues responding beyond that, saturating only beyond 450 µlL⁻¹.
  leads to: CO₂ limitation depends on both the plant pathway and the availability of light.

  terms: ★ carbon dioxide enriched atmosphere = ऐसी हवा जिसमें CO₂ की मात्रा बढ़ाई गई हो; yield
  why: The grower wants more useful crop production. → C₃ crops respond to higher CO₂ with increased photosynthesis. → Tomatoes and bell pepper grown in enriched greenhouse air give higher yields.

  exam: Book examples: tomatoes and bell pepper; link CO₂ enrichment to C₃ productivity.

- c24 CO₂ concentration and greenhouse enrichment (comparison): Basis: book. The text gives atmospheric CO₂ as 0.03–0.04%, describes stimulation up to 0.05% with possible long-term damage beyond it, and identifies CO₂ as a major limiting factor; these are the book's values rather than a claim about present measurements. Neither C₃ nor C₄ plants responds to added CO₂ under low light, whereas under high light C₄ saturation occurs near 360 µL L⁻¹ and C₃ saturation beyond 450 µL L⁻¹, explaining CO₂-enriched greenhouse production of tomatoes and bell pepper. · items p020-i3, p020-i4, p020-i5
- c25 Temperature, habitat and indirect water-stress effects (cause_effect): Basis: book. Enzymatic carbon reactions are strongly temperature-controlled, while light reactions are less temperature-sensitive; C₄ plants have higher temperature optima than C₃ plants, and tropical plants higher optima than temperate-adapted plants. Water affects photosynthesis chiefly through plant condition: stress closes stomata and restricts CO₂, while wilting reduces leaf surface area and metabolic activity, rather than acting simply through a shortage of photolysis substrate. · items p020-i7, p020-i8, p020-i9, p020-i11

## Exam focus

- c02 Experimental requirements: chlorophyll, light and carbon dioxide: Controlled starch comparisons test how evidence establishes chlorophyll, light and CO₂ requirements.
- c03 Priestley and Ingenhousz: restoration of air and oxygen release: Match investigators, experimental conditions, observations and conclusions without confusing air restoration with proof of light dependence.
- c04 Sachs and Engelmann: carbohydrate production and action spectrum: Engelmann's organism, aerobic-bacterial indicator and blue/red accumulation connect historical experiments with action spectra.
- c05 Hydrogen donors, oxygen source and overall equations: Water-derived oxygen, donor differences and the twelve-water overall equation are important statement and equation distinctions.
- c06 Chloroplast location, structure and division of labour: Chloroplast labels, membrane-versus-stroma functions and the misleading 'dark reaction' name support location-based questions.
- c07 Leaf pigments and their chromatographic colours: Chromatographic pigment names and colours are direct classification facts.
- c08 Absorption spectra, action spectra and accessory-pigment functions: Distinguish absorption from action spectra, read blue/red peaks and explain accessory pigments and incomplete overlap.
- c09 Light-harvesting complexes and reaction centres: P700/P680, antenna versus reaction centre and discovery order versus functioning order are precise contrasts.
- c10 Non-cyclic electron transport and the Z scheme: Trace PS II-to-PS I electron flow, excitation events, final acceptor and Z-scheme interpretation.
- c11 Water splitting supplies replacement electrons: Photolysis stoichiometry, PS II association, electron replacement and lumen-side proton release are tightly linked facts.
- c12 Cyclic versus non-cyclic photophosphorylation: Compare photosystems, electron return, products, membrane locations and wavelength conditions in cyclic and non-cyclic flow.
- c13 Chemiosmosis: proton gradient and ATP synthase: Three causes of the proton gradient, lumen pH, proton-return direction and CF₀/CF₁ locations test mechanistic understanding.
- c14 Calvin's investigation and indirect light dependence of biosynthesis: ¹⁴C tracing, PGA identification and indirect light dependence distinguish investigation, product and energy source.
- c15 C₃ and C₄ classification and the unexpected RuBP acceptor: Do not confuse five-carbon RuBP with three-carbon PGA or three-carbon PEP with four-carbon OAA.
- c16 Calvin-cycle stages and carbon flow: Calvin-cycle stages, RuBisCO action and stage-specific ATP/NADPH use support pathway and diagram questions.
- c17 Calvin-cycle energy budget and supplementary cyclic ATP: Calculate three ATP/two NADPH per CO₂ and eighteen ATP/twelve NADPH per glucose over six turns.
- c18 C₄ adaptations and Kranz anatomy: Kranz anatomy, bundle sheath characteristics and maize/sorghum examples are central structural discriminators.
- c19 Hatch and Slack pathway and cellular compartmentation: PEPcase/RuBisCO distribution, two-cell transport, decarboxylation and acceptor regeneration distinguish C₄ fixation from Calvin fixation.
- c20 RuBisCO competition, photorespiration and C₄ productivity: RuBisCO competition, oxygenation products, photorespiratory costs and C₄ CO₂ concentration explain productivity differences.
- c21 Integrated C₃–C₄ comparison and table practice: The seventeen-row table integrates the chapter's major C₃–C₄ distinctions and preserves the supplied answer choices.
- c22 Internal and external factors and Blackman's law: Apply Blackman's law to interacting factors rather than treating each requirement independently.
- c23 Light response, saturation and graph interpretation: Interpret rising and plateau regions, saturation projections, ten-per-cent sunlight saturation and excessive-light damage.
- c24 CO₂ concentration and greenhouse enrichment: Recall the book's CO₂ ranges, C₃/C₄ saturation contrasts and greenhouse crop examples in the correct light context.

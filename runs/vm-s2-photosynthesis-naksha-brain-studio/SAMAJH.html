Photosynthesis uses light energy to synthesise organic compounds and releases oxygen derived from water. Experiments establish its requirements; chloroplast membranes capture energy through photosystems, electron transport and chemiosmosis, while stromal reactions use ATP and NADPH to fix carbon. The Calvin cycle, C₄ carbon concentration, photorespiration and limiting factors together explain variation in photosynthetic efficiency and productivity.

c10 Non-cyclic electron transport and the Z scheme process
Basis: book. Light excites P680 electrons to an acceptor; carriers pass them downhill to PS I, where light excites electrons again and a second acceptor pathway ultimately reduces NADP⁺ to NADPH. Electrons are transferred rather than used up, and the two light-driven rises and intervening descents produce the Z-shaped arrangement on a redox-potential scale; non-cyclic flow operates PS II then PS I and supports ATP and NADPH formation.
c11 Water splitting supplies replacement electrons process
Basis: book. Electrons lost from PS II are replaced by its associated water-splitting complex, while PS II supplies replacement electrons to PS I through the transport chain. The reaction 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + O₂ + 4e⁻ releases protons on the lumen-facing side of the thylakoid membrane and establishes water as the source of evolved oxygen.
c12 Cyclic versus non-cyclic photophosphorylation comparison
Basis: book. Photophosphorylation forms ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate using light; in cyclic flow, excited PS I electrons return through carriers to P700 instead of reducing NADP⁺, producing ATP without NADPH. Contrast this with the PS II-to-PS I non-cyclic pathway; the book identifies stromal lamellae lacking PS II and NADP reductase as a possible cyclic-flow location and also associates cyclic flow with excitation wavelengths beyond 680 nm.
c13 Chemiosmosis: proton gradient and ATP synthase cause_effect
Basis: book. Water oxidation adds lumen protons, hydrogen-carrier transport moves protons from stroma to lumen, and stromal NADP⁺ reduction removes stromal protons, producing high lumen H⁺, lower lumen pH and an electrochemical gradient. In the figure, follow PS II → PQ/PQH₂ → cytochrome B6f → PC → PS I → Fd → FNR, separately from proton movement; proton return through membrane CF₀ drives conformational changes in stromal CF₁ and ATP synthesis, requiring a membrane, proton pump, gradient and ATP synthase.
c14 Calvin's investigation and indirect light dependence of biosynthesis process
Basis: book. Light-reaction oxygen diffuses out, while ATP and NADPH drive stromal sugar synthesis; biosynthesis briefly continues after light removal and then stops, demonstrating indirect light dependence rather than a requirement for darkness. Calvin, born in Minnesota in April 1911, earned his chemistry Ph.D. there and worked at Berkeley; his post-war work with Bassham using ¹⁴CO₂ identified three-carbon PGA and mapped carbon assimilation, earning the 1961 Nobel Prize, while his electron-transfer ideas and photosynthetic principles connect to solar-energy and renewable-resource research.
c15 C₃ and C₄ classification and the unexpected RuBP acceptor classification
Basis: book. C₃ plants have three-carbon PGA as the first stable fixation product, whereas C₄ plants initially form four-carbon OAA; this classification concerns the initial stable product, not whether the Calvin cycle exists. The Calvin-cycle acceptor is five-carbon ketose RuBP, not the initially suspected two-carbon compound, and its regeneration makes a cycle shared by photosynthetic plants including C₄ plants.
c16 Calvin-cycle stages and carbon flow process
Basis: book. Carboxylation uses RuBisCO to combine CO₂ with RuBP and form two 3-PGA molecules; reduction uses ATP and NADPH to produce carbohydrate intermediates, and regeneration uses ATP to restore RuBP. Trace the figure's triose-phosphate branch toward sucrose and starch separately from its return to RuBP, and distinguish the two ATP plus two NADPH used in reduction per CO₂ from the additional ATP used in regeneration.
c17 Calvin-cycle energy budget and supplementary cyclic ATP facts_numbers
Basis: book. Each CO₂ fixed requires three ATP and two NADPH; six turns fix six CO₂ to produce the glucose-equivalent output, consuming eighteen ATP and twelve NADPH and returning eighteen ADP and twelve oxidised NADP carriers. The book proposes that cyclic phosphorylation probably helps meet the unequal ATP and NADPH requirements; teach this as its suggested explanation, not a demonstrated fixed coupling ratio.
c18 C₄ adaptations and Kranz anatomy structure
Basis: book. C₄ plants adapted to dry tropical conditions have distinctive anatomy, high-light and higher-temperature responses, greater biomass productivity and, in the chapter's account, no photorespiration. Kranz means wreath: large chloroplast-rich bundle sheath cells surround vascular bundles, may form several layers, have thick walls impervious to gaseous exchange and lack intercellular spaces; compare vertical sections, including maize or sorghum, rather than relying on external appearance.
c19 Hatch and Slack pathway and cellular compartmentation process
Basis: book. In C₄ mesophyll cells, three-carbon PEP accepts inorganic carbon through PEPcase to form OAA, followed by malic or aspartic acid; C₄ acids move to bundle sheath cells, release CO₂ for the Calvin cycle, and return a three-carbon compound for PEP regeneration. The figure shows bicarbonate input, cell boundaries and plasmodesmatal transport; distinguish PEPcase-containing mesophyll lacking RuBisCO from RuBisCO-rich bundle sheath lacking PEPcase, whereas C₃ Calvin fixation occurs in mesophyll.
c20 RuBisCO competition, photorespiration and C₄ productivity cause_effect
Basis: book. RuBisCO, described as the most abundant enzyme, competitively binds CO₂ or O₂; normal RuBP carboxylation yields two PGA molecules, whereas oxygenation yields one phosphoglycerate and a two-carbon phosphoglycolate, reducing carbon fixation. Photorespiration consumes ATP and releases CO₂ without producing sugars, ATP or NADPH; C₄-acid decarboxylation raises bundle sheath CO₂ around RuBisCO, minimises oxygenase activity and explains the chapter's absence of C₄ photorespiration and higher productivity.

c22 Internal and external factors and Blackman's law cause_effect
Basis: book. Internal controls include leaf number, size, age and orientation, mesophyll and chloroplast characteristics, internal CO₂ and chlorophyll, linked to genetic predisposition and growth; external controls include light, temperature, CO₂ and water. Blackman's 1905 law identifies the factor nearest its minimum as the one whose change directly changes rate, explaining why optimal light and CO₂ cannot overcome very low temperature.
c23 Light response, saturation and graph interpretation cause_effect
Basis: book and supplied figure description. Distinguish light quality, intensity and duration: fixation rises approximately linearly at low intensity, bends as other factors limit it, and reaches a plateau; the book states saturation around ten per cent of full sunlight and warns that sufficiently excessive light breaks down chlorophyll and decreases photosynthesis. In Figure 11.10, A marks the rising region, B the transition and C the plateau; D projects saturation-region intensity onto the horizontal axis and E projects plateau rate onto the vertical axis, so answer the exercise from those actual labels.
c24 CO₂ concentration and greenhouse enrichment comparison
Basis: book. The text gives atmospheric CO₂ as 0.03–0.04%, describes stimulation up to 0.05% with possible long-term damage beyond it, and identifies CO₂ as a major limiting factor; these are the book's values rather than a claim about present measurements. Neither C₃ nor C₄ plants responds to added CO₂ under low light, whereas under high light C₄ saturation occurs near 360 µL L⁻¹ and C₃ saturation beyond 450 µL L⁻¹, explaining CO₂-enriched greenhouse production of tomatoes and bell pepper.
c25 Temperature, habitat and indirect water-stress effects cause_effect
Basis: book. Enzymatic carbon reactions are strongly temperature-controlled, while light reactions are less temperature-sensitive; C₄ plants have higher temperature optima than C₃ plants, and tropical plants higher optima than temperate-adapted plants. Water affects photosynthesis chiefly through plant condition: stress closes stomata and restricts CO₂, while wilting reduces leaf surface area and metabolic activity, rather than acting simply through a shortage of photolysis substrate.

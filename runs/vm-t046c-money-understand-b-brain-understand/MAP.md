## Concepts

### Meaning, characteristics and four functions of money · definition

Book: Money is anything accepted for goods, services and debt settlement, including cash, deposits and digital balances; its functions are medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value and standard of deferred payment, illustrated by ₹500 groceries, a ₹20,000 phone versus ₹60,000 laptop, bank savings and home-loan EMIs. Book characteristics, with Outside textbook explanation: acceptability lets recipients reuse money; durability supports repeated use and storage; portability makes payment convenient; divisibility permits small payments and change; uniformity makes equivalent units interchangeable; limited supply helps protect value against excessive issuance relative to demand. Uniformity does not mean different denominations have equal face values, and scarcity alone does not guarantee stable purchasing power.

### Barter and the problems money solves · cause_effect

Book: Barter directly exchanges goods and services without money, values are mutually agreed, and the system was common in early civilizations but survives in limited informal exchanges. Book limitations, with Outside explanatory mapping: a medium of exchange removes double coincidence of wants; a unit of account supplies a common value measure; divisible money avoids dividing an indivisible good; a store of value facilitates retaining purchasing power; a standard of deferred payment expresses future debts in agreed monetary units. Outside example: a wheat seller receives money from someone who does not sell cloth, then buys cloth elsewhere rather than finding a cloth seller who wants wheat.

### Intrinsic versus extrinsic value · comparison

Book: Intrinsic value rests on an item's own material or usefulness and remains outside monetary use, whereas extrinsic monetary value rests on authority and trust rather than inherent material worth; examples contrast gold, silver, salt, cattle and cowries with notes, deposits and digital balances, including bullion, rupee notes, bank balances and UPI in context. Book paired differences: commodity-money acceptance is limited by utility and availability versus wider acceptance through trust and legal recognition; commodity/metallic money is described as generally durable whereas paper can perish and digital balances depend on technology; division into small payment units and carrying bulky/heavy commodities are described as difficult versus easier denominations and transfers for paper/digital forms. Outside explanation and qualifications: useful material can retain non-monetary value, so the table's 'nil value' means negligible non-monetary worth, not zero purchasing power; its comparisons are broad tendencies—metallic coins can be more divisible and portable than cattle or other commodities, and deposits or UPI do not become legal tender merely because widely accepted.

## Prerequisites to teach first

- **Cell as a unit of life and specialised cell functions** (outside): Explain that cells perform life functions and can specialise, providing the starting point for tissues, division of labour and the chapter's specialised cell examples.
- **Cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus and genetic material** (outside): Teach the boundary, internal material and genetic-control structure so that living cell contents, nuclear labels and distinctions between whole cells and cell parts are meaningful.
- **Part–whole hierarchy and organs working together** (outside): Explain that a larger functional unit can contain smaller units and that cooperating organs form a system; this makes the biological-organisation arrows intelligible.
- **Chromosome sets, homologous pairs and sister chromatids** (outside): Distinguish diploid from haploid, homologous chromosomes from duplicated chromatids, and chromosome number from DNA amount before interpreting division outcomes and crossing over.
- **DNA replication in S phase and chromosome counting by centromeres** (outside): Teach that replication copies DNA before division while duplicated chromatids remain associated, enabling a precise explanation of the erroneous chromosome-doubling row.
- **Nuclear division, cytokinesis, spindle, centrioles and asters** (outside): Separate division of the nucleus from division of cytoplasm and introduce the chromosome-moving apparatus before comparing phase counts, cell plates and cleavage.
- **Somatic cells, reproductive cell lineage, gametes and spores** (outside): Explain growth and replacement versus sexual reproduction, and distinguish plant spore formation from direct gamete formation when qualifying the mitosis–meiosis table.
- **Classification trees and parent–subgroup relationships** (outside): Show how to follow branches without confusing a parent tissue class, one of its types and a constituent cell; this also supports exception questions.
- **Plant growth regions, differentiation and apical, lateral and intercalary positions** (outside): Introduce root and shoot tips, girth, nodes, internodes and leaf bases, then explain division versus differentiation so that meristem names and the permanent-tissue branch have concrete meaning.
- **Primary and secondary cell walls, cellulose, lignin and pits** (outside): Explain the initial wall, later inner thickening, wall materials and wall pits before using wall thickness and lignification to distinguish tissues and xylem cells.
- **Vacuole, lumen and intercellular space** (outside): Locate the vacuole within a living cell, the lumen inside a wall and spaces between cells; distinguish these while comparing tissue tables, narrow sclerenchyma cavities and aerenchyma.
- **Plant anatomical locations used in the occurrence tables** (outside): Locate stem, root, leaf, petiole, epidermis, hypodermis, cortex, pith, medullary rays, vascular bundle, bundle sheath and pericycle, and distinguish dicots from monocots before memorising tissue locations.
- **Food storage, chloroplasts and photosynthesis** (outside): Explain that stored food differs from food production and that photosynthesis requires chloroplasts, supporting parenchyma functions and the chlorenchyma distractor.
- **Water, dissolved minerals and vertical versus sideways transport** (outside): Introduce materials moving within plants and directions relative to the plant axis so that tubular conduction and parenchyma's lateral role are understood.
- **Sieve-tube element, sieve plate and companion-cell support** (outside): Explain how successive conducting elements meet at porous end walls and how an adjacent living companion cell supports them before interpreting the detailed phloem labels and nucleus position.
- **Mechanical support, tensile strength, stress and strain** (outside): Explain resistance to pulling and deformation so that wall structure can be connected to flexible support, rigid strength and protection.
- **Muscle contraction, voluntary control and involuntary control** (outside): Explain contraction as force production and distinguish deliberate control from activity occurring without deliberate command before comparing muscular tissues.
- **Skeleton, heart pump, digestive tract, blood vessels, uterus, pupil and hair muscles** (outside): Locate the structures named in the muscle table and explain peristalsis, circulation, posture and pupil adjustment sufficiently to connect muscle location with function.
- **Extracellular matrix, fibres and cells embedded in matrix** (outside): Introduce material outside cells and distinguish a cell from its surrounding matrix and fibres before interpreting connective-tissue panels.
- **Blood, oxygen transport, defence and clotting** (outside): Explain the broad roles that distinguish red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets without requiring unprovided numerical counts or detailed cell functions.
- **Phagocytosis, inflammation, antibodies and plasma cells** (outside): Teach engulfing material, inflammatory responses and antibody production to make the labelled defensive cells in blood and areolar tissue understandable.
- **Bone cells, cartilage cells and cell processes** (outside): Introduce osteocyte and chondrocyte as cell names and a cell process as an extension, enabling correct interpretation of canaliculi and cartilage matrix.
- **Lipids as stored food and displacement within a cell** (outside): Explain why a large lipid droplet can occupy much of an adipocyte and position its nucleus near the edge.
- **Nerve impulse, cell-membrane ions and chemical synapse** (outside): Introduce an electrical change travelling along a neuron and chemical signalling across a synapse so the arrow pathway can be converted into an accurate stepwise explanation.
- **Buoyancy, density and air-filled spaces in aquatic plants** (outside): Explain how air spaces can lower average tissue density and aid floating before identifying aerenchyma from the question.
- **Reading diagram labels, sections and directional arrows** (outside): Distinguish a leader identifying a part from an arrow showing motion, recognise schematic sections and follow panel letters across a compound figure.
- **Evaluating numbered statements and negative question stems** (outside): Practise judging each statement separately, interpreting 'except' and 'incorrect', and recognising that options alone cannot establish an answer when a stem is missing.

## Concepts

### Tissue and the plant–animal distinction · definition

Book basis: A tissue is formed when cells combine to perform the same function; plant cells form plant tissues and animal cells form animal tissues. Use the shared function to explain why a tissue is a functional group of cells rather than simply a collection of neighbouring cells.

Items: p001-i2, p001-i3, p001-i4

### Division of labour and biological organisation · cause_effect

Book basis: Tissue formation causes division of labour, reduces the workload on individual cells and leads to organisation. Read the diagram as a hierarchy of parts within larger wholes: Cell → Tissues → Organ → Organ system → Organisms, with tissues between cells and organs and organ systems between organs and the organism. Outside clarification: This is a hierarchy of organisation, not a claim that an individual cell passes through these stages during its life.

### Mitosis and meiosis: site, divisions and outcomes · comparison

Book basis: The table contrasts mitosis in somatic cells with meiosis in germ cells, one nuclear division with two, two daughter cells with four, diploid daughters with haploid daughters, more frequent with less frequent occurrence, somatic-organ formation with gamete formation, and unchanged chromosome number with reduction to half; it prints 'Equation division' against 'Reduction division'. Outside clarification: Read 'Equation' as equational: mitosis generally preserves the starting chromosome-set number and is not restricted to diploid starting cells, whereas meiosis reduces the chromosome-set number; frequency depends on the cells and organism. Outside clarification: 'Germ cells' here means the reproductive cell lineage, and meiosis produces spores rather than gametes directly in plant life cycles.

### Mitosis and meiosis: phases, replication and crossing over · comparison

Book basis: Mitosis has one prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase; meiosis has two of each and five subphases in prophase I, with crossing over contrasted as absent in mitosis and present in meiosis. Book basis: The replication row says chromosome number doubles at the beginning of each mitotic division and, for meiosis, after the first meiotic division; retain this as the printed claim requiring correction. Outside clarification: DNA replicates before mitosis and before meiosis I, without doubling chromosome number when chromosomes are counted by centromeres, and there is no intervening DNA replication between meiosis I and II; meiotic crossing over involves non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes in prophase I.

Items: p001-i20, p001-i22, p001-i23

### Plant and animal mitosis · comparison

Book basis: The plant column lists absent centrioles, no asters, cell-plate formation and division mainly at meristems; the animal column lists present centrioles, asters, furrowing and cleavage of cytoplasm, and division in tissues throughout the body. Compare the division apparatus, separation of cytoplasm and location of division as separate criteria. Outside clarification: The plant description is the usual higher-plant comparison, while animal division throughout the body does not mean that each mature animal cell retains the ability to divide.

### Simple tissues compared: shape, walls and living contents · comparison

Book basis: Parenchyma has isodiametric oval, spherical or polygonal cells, thin cellulosic walls, abundant cytoplasm, nuclei, a large vacuole and intercellular spaces; collenchyma has circular, oval or polyhedral cells, uneven wall thickening, cytoplasm, nuclei and vacuoles, with spaces listed as absent. Sclerenchyma has variable forms including fibres and sclereids, lignified secondary walls, and absent cytoplasm, nuclei, vacuoles and intercellular spaces; the table identifies parenchyma and collenchyma as living and sclerenchyma as dead. Outside clarification: A vacuole is inside a cell, an intercellular space is between cells, and the lumen is the cavity enclosed by a cell wall, so these should be compared separately.

Items: p013-i9, p013-i10, p013-i11, p013-i12, p013-i13, p013-i14

### Simple tissues compared: occurrence and functions · comparison

Book basis: Parenchyma is described as packing tissue in soft plant parts, pith, cortex and medullary rays, with food storage and photosynthesis; collenchyma occurs in dicot stems, petiole and beneath the epidermis, is listed as absent in monocots and roots, and provides tensile strength, mechanical support and photosynthesis. Sclerenchyma is listed in dicot hypodermis, bundle sheath, pericycle, seed and fruit pulp, with protection from stress and strain and mechanical strength. Outside clarification: Photosynthesis in parenchyma or collenchyma depends on chloroplasts being present, and the table's occurrence statements should be taught as its stated generalisations.

### Sclerenchyma: occurrence, lignified walls and strength · structure

Book basis: Sclerenchyma occurs around stem vascular bundles, in leaf veins and hard seed and nut coverings, with coconut husk as an example; its cells are described as long, narrow and dead, with lignin-thickened walls and absent intercellular spaces, providing protection and mechanical strength. Book basis: The text attributes an absent cell cavity to excessive wall thickness, although the supplied page 13 drawing shows narrow cavities. Outside clarification: Thick lignified walls can greatly reduce the lumen, and 'long and narrow' particularly describes fibres; sclereids account for the variable forms noted elsewhere in the book.

### Complex permanent tissue: coordinated cell types · structure

Book basis: Complex permanent tissue contains more than one type of cell, with the cells coordinating to perform a common function. Its stated transport materials are water, minerals and food, and its two types are xylem and phloem. Outside clarification: Distinguish this from a simple tissue by the variety of constituent cell types, rather than by whether a tissue performs an important or complicated function.

### Xylem components and their work · structure

Book basis: The cropped classification tree visibly lists Tracheids, Vessels (Tracheae) and Xylem parenchyma, while the later text gives the complete supplied list of tracheids, vessels, xylem parenchyma and xylem fibres. Tracheids and vessels have thick walls, many are dead at maturity, and their tubular form permits vertical transport of water and minerals. Xylem parenchyma stores food and helps sideways conduction of water, whereas xylem fibres mainly provide support.

### Three muscular tissues: location, function, appearance and control · comparison

Book basis: Skeletal muscle is associated with the skeleton, produces movement, heat and posture, and has parallel striated fibres with multiple eccentric nuclei under voluntary control; cardiac muscle occurs in the heart, pumps blood continuously, and is striated with one central nucleus under involuntary control. Visceral or smooth muscle is listed in the G.I. tract, uterus, eye and blood vessels, with peristalsis, blood-pressure control, pupil-size control and hair erection among its functions, and it lacks striations, has one central nucleus and is involuntary. Outside clarification: Treat these functions as a set across smooth-muscle locations, rather than assigning each function to each listed organ; eccentric nuclei lie toward the cell periphery.

## Concepts

### Tissues, division of labour and levels of organisation · structure

Book: Cells combining to perform the same function form a tissue; plant cells form plant tissues and animal cells form animal tissues. Grouping work into tissues produces division of labour, reduces the workload on individual cells and supports organisation into Cell → Tissues → Organ → Organ system → Organisms. Read these arrows as component-to-larger-whole relationships, not as stages in one cell's life.

### Mitosis versus meiosis · comparison

Book: Mitosis is compared with meiosis by somatic versus germ-cell occurrence, one versus two nuclear divisions, two versus four daughter cells, diploid versus haploid products, greater versus lesser frequency, somatic-organ versus gamete formation, one versus two sets of division phases, and five subphases of meiotic prophase I. ### Plant versus animal mitosis · comparison

Book: Plant mitosis is presented as lacking centrioles and asters, dividing the cytoplasm through a cell plate, and occurring mainly at meristems; animal mitosis has centrioles and asters, cytoplasmic furrowing and cleavage, and distribution through body tissues. Outside clarification: This is the usual higher-plant versus animal comparison rather than an exception-free rule for all plants or all animal cells; a cell plate builds a separating partition, whereas cleavage constricts the animal cell.

### Epithelial sheets, barriers and gland formation · structure

Book: Epithelium covers most organs and cavities, forming a continuous sheet of tightly packed cells with little cementing material and almost no intercellular spaces; an extracellular fibrous basement membrane usually separates it from underlying tissue. This arrangement forms a barrier between body systems. Epithelial cells may specialise into surface-secreting gland cells, and inward folding of an epithelial portion can form a multicellular gland called glandular epithelium.

### Epithelial types: shape, layers, locations and functions · comparison

Book: Simple squamous is a single flat, irregular-boundaried layer in blood vessels, capillaries, lung air sacs and Bowman's capsule for diffusion with minimal protection; simple cuboidal lines kidney tubules and gland ducts for absorption and secretion, with microvilli mentioned in proximal convoluted tubules; simple columnar is tall and slender, often ciliated, in digestive and upper respiratory linings for protection, absorption, mucus secretion and directional movement. Stratified squamous protects mouth and vagina through renewed layers whose older cells flatten above an active columnar basal layer; stratified cuboidal protects mammary, sweat and salivary gland ducts; stratified columnar protects and secretes in the male urethra and lobar salivary ducts; pseudostratified columnar has unequal cell heights in respiratory passages and gland ducts for protection, secretion and mucus movement. Keratinised outer cells are described as dead and lacking nucleus and cytoplasm, with keratin providing waterproofing and abrasion protection; transitional urothelium in bladder, urethra and ureter changes from cuboidal-looking to flattened irregular cells during stretching, accommodating liquid volume and forming a tight-junction barrier—outside clarification: cuboidal cells are approximately cube-shaped, pseudostratification is apparent rather than true layering, and urothelium lines only part of the urethra.

### Connective tissue: matrix, fibres and mechanical properties · structure

Book: Connective tissue supports, joins, binds and packs body parts, helping prevent organ displacement during movement; relatively few loosely spaced cells lie within an intercellular matrix that may be jelly-like, fluid, dense or rigid. Cells secrete a polysaccharide gel and protein fibres: widespread collagen is flexible with high tensile strength, elastin forms stretchable networks that recoil, and thin reticulate fibres containing collagen and glycoproteins form delicate networks joining neighbouring tissues. In the diagram, distinguish ground substance and collagen grouped as extracellular matrix from mesenchymal cells, macrophage and adipocytes, and identify thick collagen fibres, finer elastic fibres and the blood vessel.

### Connective-tissue varieties, locations and functions · comparison

Book: Loose connective tissue supports and provides elasticity in deep skin layers and around vessels, nerves and organs; dense tissue in tendons and ligaments strongly packs structures together; elastic tissue supplies elasticity in lungs, arteries, trachea and vocal cords. Book: Muscular tissue consists of large elongated muscle cells, also called muscle fibres, containing contractile proteins and carrying out contraction and relaxation. ### Smooth or visceral muscle · structure

Book: Smooth fibres are unbranched spindle-shaped or fusiform cells with tapering ends, one central nucleus and no striations; they are also called unstriated or involuntary muscles. ### Cardiac muscle · structure

Book: Cardiac muscle consists of cylindrical, branched, striated fibres, each described as uninucleate, and is involuntary. ### Comparing skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle · comparison

Book: Skeletal muscle lies at the skeleton, produces movement, heat and posture, and has parallel striated fibres with multiple eccentric nuclei under voluntary control; cardiac muscle lies in the heart, pumps continuously, and is striated with one central nucleus under involuntary control. Visceral smooth muscle occurs in the gastrointestinal tract, uterus, eye and blood vessels, has one central nucleus without striations, and acts involuntarily in peristalsis, blood-pressure regulation, pupil size and hair erection. The three drawings distinguish a long unbranched multinucleate cylinder, branching striped cardiac fibres, and a tapered smooth cell; striation and voluntary control must therefore be treated as different criteria.

Book: Meristematic tissue occurs in growing root and shoot regions; its highly active dividing cells are spherical, oval or rectangular, compactly packed without intercellular spaces, and described as lacking vacuoles. ### Simple permanent tissue and parenchyma specialisations · classification

Book: Simple permanent tissue contains one structurally and functionally similar cell type; its introductory functions include food storage and photosynthesis. Parenchyma occurs in stems, roots, leaves, flowers and fruits, with thin-walled round, polygonal or elongated cells separated by intercellular spaces, and acts as food-storage tissue. ### Collenchyma: flexible mechanical support · cause_effect

Book: Collenchyma occurs in leaf stalks and dicot stems and consists of living elongated cells with irregular corner thickening and very little intercellular space. It supplies mechanical strength while permitting flexibility and bending, with photosynthesis also listed among its functions. Tendrils and climber stems illustrate why support must sometimes resist pulling without making a growing organ rigid.

### Sclerenchyma: lignified strength and protection · cause_effect

Book: Sclerenchyma occurs around stem vascular bundles, in leaf veins and hard seed and nut coverings, with coconut husk as an example; its dead cells lack intercellular spaces and have thick lignified walls that provide strength and protection. ### Complex permanent tissue and xylem components · structure

Book: Complex permanent tissue contains multiple cell types coordinating a common function, especially transport of water, minerals and food, and has two types, xylem and phloem. Xylem comprises tracheids, vessels, xylem parenchyma and xylem fibres: thick-walled tubular tracheids and vessels, many dead when mature, conduct water and minerals vertically; parenchyma stores food and assists sideways water conduction; fibres mainly provide support. Outside clarification: xylem parenchyma is living, so the presence of dead conducting elements does not make the whole tissue exclusively dead.

### Phloem components and food transport · structure

Book: The phloem list includes five cell types—sieve cells, sieve tubes, companion cells, phloem fibres and phloem parenchyma—and describes sieve tubes as tubular cells with perforated walls. Phloem carries food from leaves to other plant parts, and its cells are living except for phloem fibres. Outside clarification: sieve cells and sieve-tube elements are conducting types associated with different plant groups, so the list is a general inventory rather than a requirement that every phloem sample contain all five.

## Prerequisites to teach first

- **Cells, tissues, organs and body systems** (outside): Teach a cell as a structural unit, a tissue as an organised group of cells, an organ as a structure containing tissues, and a body system as cooperating organs. This supplies the organisational scale needed for the four tissue categories and their locations.
- **Cell membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus** (outside): Teach the cell membrane as the cell boundary, cytoplasm as the material within it outside the nucleus, and the nucleus as the genetic-control compartment. Learners must distinguish whole cells, internal parts, multiple nuclei and the neuron's signalling route.
- **Covering, lining, cavities and compartments** (outside): Teach covering as lying over a surface, lining as covering an internal surface, and a cavity as an internal space. Explain a compartment as a separated body region so that epithelial barrier functions have a concrete meaning.
- **Cell shapes, layers and tissue surfaces** (outside): Teach flattened, cube-like, tall slender, cylindrical and spindle-shaped forms, and contrast one cell layer with several layers. Identify the free surface, the lower or basal region, and central versus peripheral positions before interpreting diagrams.
- **Extracellular material, intercellular space and fibres** (outside): Teach extracellular as outside cells, intercellular as between cells, matrix as surrounding extracellular material, and fibres as thread-like extracellular components. Explain a cell process as an extension of a cell; this distinguishes epithelial cementing material, basement membrane, connective matrix and bone-cell processes.
- **Diffusion across a thin barrier** (outside): Teach diffusion as net movement down a concentration gradient and explain that a shorter path can facilitate exchange. This makes the single thin squamous layer's association with diffusion understandable.
- **Absorption, secretion, glands and ducts** (outside): Teach absorption as taking substances across a surface, secretion as release of a substance from a cell, a gland as a secretory structure, and a duct as a passage carrying glandular output. Explain multicellular as composed of multiple cells.
- **Cilia, microvilli and mucus** (outside): Teach cilia as hair-like surface projections whose coordinated beating can move material, microvilli as small projections increasing surface area, and mucus as a slippery secretion. These definitions prevent confusion between surface-area enhancement and directed surface transport.
- **Blood vessels, capillaries and lung air sacs** (outside): Teach blood vessels as blood-carrying passages, capillaries as small exchange vessels, and lung air sacs as gas-exchange spaces. These are the concrete sites needed to learn the simple squamous row.
- **Kidney tubules, proximal convoluted tubules and Bowman's capsule** (outside): Teach Bowman's capsule as the cup-like beginning of a kidney filtration unit and kidney tubules as passages involved in processing the filtrate, with the proximal convoluted tubule as an early coiled segment. Keep capsule lining and tubular lining distinct when learning the epithelial examples.
- **Digestive and respiratory passages, urethra and named glands** (outside): Locate the digestive passage, upper respiratory passage, male urethra, mammary glands, sweat glands and salivary glands at a simple anatomical level. Explain that lobar ducts are ducts associated with gland lobes, so the table's location labels can be understood rather than memorised as unfamiliar words.
- **Cell division, metabolism, shedding and abrasion** (outside): Teach cell division as production of new cells, metabolism as cellular chemical activity, shedding as loss of surface cells, and abrasion as surface wear. These ideas explain renewal of the protective stratified squamous lining.
- **Muscle contraction, muscle fibres and striations** (outside): Teach contraction as force generation that can produce shortening or tension, muscle fibre as the elongated muscle-cell unit in this context, and striations as visible transverse bands. This supplies the vocabulary for appearance and movement without requiring molecular contraction mechanisms.
- **Voluntary and involuntary control** (outside): Teach voluntary control as conscious control of an action and involuntary activity as activity that does not ordinarily require a conscious command. Use these meanings before comparing the muscle rows.
- **Skeleton, heart circulation and smooth-muscle target structures** (outside): Locate the skeleton and heart, explain that the heart pumps blood, identify the uterus and the pupil as the eye's opening, and define blood pressure as pressure exerted by blood within vessels. Define peristalsis as a coordinated travelling contraction so the muscle functions can be interpreted.
- **Blood components and their broad roles** (outside): Distinguish white blood cells associated with defence, red blood cells associated with oxygen transport, and platelets associated with clotting at damaged vessels. Treat these as explanatory foundations, not as functions printed beside the supplied blood-cell labels.
- **Connective-tissue cell names and matrix vocabulary** (outside): Teach osteocyte as a bone cell, chondrocyte as a cartilage cell, and adipocyte as a fat-storage cell. Introduce collagen and reticular fibres as extracellular network components and fibroblast, macrophage, mast cell and plasma cell as distinct cell names, avoiding the assumption that fibre labels name cells.
- **Phagocytosis, inflammation and antibodies as optional vocabulary support** (outside): If immune terms are used to explain the labelled cells, first teach phagocytosis as engulfment of material, inflammation as a response to injury or infection, and antibodies as proteins involved in specific defence; plasma cells are antibody-secreting cells derived from B lymphocytes. These are outside clarifications, not additional functions asserted by the supplied areolar or blood-cell diagrams.
- **Lipids, fat storage and nuclear position** (outside): Teach fat as a lipid stored within cells and usable as an energy reserve. Explain that a large intracellular droplet can occupy much of the cell interior, preparing learners to interpret the adipocyte's marginal nucleus.
- **Stimulus and a nerve impulse** (outside): Teach a stimulus as a detectable change and a nerve impulse as an electrical signal travelling along a neuron's membrane. Explain that ions are charged particles whose movement across membranes contributes to electrical signalling; detailed action-potential phases are unnecessary here.
- **Separate neurons, synaptic gaps and neurotransmitter receptors** (outside): Teach that communicating neurons remain separate cells, that a synapse is a specialised communication junction, and that neurotransmitters are chemical messengers acting on receiving-membrane receptors. This makes the transition from an electrical signal along an axon to communication with another cell understandable.
- **Reading labels, arrows and separate diagram panels** (outside): Teach pointer lines as identifiers and directional arrows as indicators of movement or sequence, while recognising that adjacent panels may simply compare examples. This prevents treating the epithelial blocks or connective-tissue panels as developmental stages.

## Concepts

### Four major body tissues and their functional identities · classification

Book basis: The body has mainly four types of tissue: epithelial, muscular, neural and connective. Their identifying functions are, respectively, covering or lining; helping movement; responding to stimuli; and supporting, linking and cushioning. Use these functions as the first classification step before examining the cells and arrangements within each group.

### Epithelial sheets, barriers and the basement membrane · structure

Book basis: Epithelium covers most organs and cavities and forms a barrier that keeps different body systems separate. Its tightly packed cells form a continuous sheet, with little cementing material and almost no intercellular spaces; it is usually separated from underlying tissue by an extracellular fibrous basement membrane. Outside explanation: The close packing helps explain the barrier function, and the basement membrane beneath the sheet is distinct from the cell membrane surrounding an individual cell.

### Glandular epithelium and inward folding · process

Book basis: Epithelial cells can acquire additional specialisation as gland cells that secrete substances at the epithelial surface. Sometimes a portion of the epithelial sheet folds inward and forms a multicellular gland; this is glandular epithelium. Outside explanation: Secretion means releasing a substance produced by a cell, while multicellular describes a structure made of multiple cells.

### Simple squamous epithelium · comparison

Book basis: Simple squamous epithelium is a single layer of flat cells with irregular boundaries, appearing in the diagram as a thin, tile-like sheet. The table locates it in blood-vessel lining, capillaries, lung air-sac lining and Bowman's capsule of the kidney, and gives transport by diffusion and situations requiring minimal protection as its functional associations. Outside explanation: A thin cellular barrier offers a short distance for diffusion, linking this structure to exchange rather than substantial protection against abrasion.

Items: p003-i13, p003-i19

### Simple cuboidal epithelium · comparison

Book basis: Simple cuboidal epithelium occurs in the tubular lining of kidneys and glandular ducts and performs absorption and secretion. The table describes a single layer of short cylindrical cells, sometimes with microvilli as in proximal convoluted tubules, whereas the diagram shows approximately cube-shaped cells. Outside explanation: Microvilli increase the cell's exposed surface area and are different from the cilia illustrated on columnar cells.

Items: p003-i15, p003-i20

### Simple columnar and ciliated columnar epithelium · comparison

Book basis: The table describes simple columnar epithelium as a single layer of tall, slender cells, often ciliated, and places it in digestive-tract and upper-respiratory-tract linings. Its listed functions are protection, absorption, mucus secretion and movement in a specific direction; the diagram shows cilia as a fringe on the free upper surface. Outside explanation: Coordinated ciliary beating can move material along a surface, whereas microvilli chiefly provide additional surface area.

Items: p003-i16, p003-i21

### Stratified squamous epithelium and renewal · process

Book basis: Stratified squamous epithelium lines the mouth and vagina and provides protection through several layers of cells. Cells are continuously sloughed off and regenerated: older cells are pushed upwards and become flat, while the lower layer is columnar and metabolically active. The diagram therefore identifies this tissue by flattened surface cells above multiple layers, not by assuming that the lower cells are also flat.

Items: p003-i14, p003-i22

### Stratified cuboidal epithelium · comparison

Book basis: Stratified cuboidal epithelium is listed in mammary glands, sweat gland and salivary glands, with protection of ducts of various glands as its function. Its upper layer is cuboid, while other layers may be cuboidal or of other types. Identify the layered arrangement and upper-cell shape together rather than assuming that each layer has the same shape.

### Stratified columnar epithelium · comparison

Book basis: Stratified columnar epithelium is listed in the male urethra and lobar ducts of salivary glands, and its functions are protection and secretion. A layer of columnar cells lies upon squamous, columnar or cuboidal epithelial cells. Compare it with stratified cuboidal epithelium by the exposed layer's shape and by the precise locations in the table.

### Three muscle types and the comparison framework · classification

Book basis: Muscular tissue helps movement, and the supplied table distinguishes skeletal, cardiac and visceral, or smooth, muscle. It compares their location, function, appearance and control, so identification requires a combination of features rather than a single visual clue. Outside explanation: Voluntary control concerns movement under conscious control, whereas involuntary activity does not ordinarily require a conscious command.

### Skeletal muscle · comparison

Book basis: Skeletal muscle is located at the skeleton and serves movement, heat and posture under voluntary control. Its parallel fibres are striated and multinucleated with eccentric nuclei; the drawing shows a long cylindrical, unbranched fibre with repeated transverse bands and several nuclei. Outside explanation: 'Eccentric' here means away from the centre, so nucleus position is an additional identifying feature.

Items: p007-i4, p007-i5

### Cardiac muscle · comparison

Book basis: Cardiac muscle is located in the heart, pumps blood continuously and is involuntary. The table describes striations and one central nucleus, while the illustration shows branching, connected striated fibres with nuclei inside them. Its combination of striations with involuntary control distinguishes it from both skeletal and smooth muscle.

Items: p007-i6, p007-i7

### Visceral or smooth muscle · comparison

Book basis: Visceral, or smooth, muscle is listed in the G.I. tract, uterus, eye and blood vessels, with functions including peristalsis, blood-pressure regulation, pupil-size control and erection of hairs. It is involuntary, lacks striations and has one central nucleus; the drawing shows a spindle-shaped cell with tapering ends and an expanded middle. Outside explanation: Peristalsis is a coordinated wave of muscular contraction that moves contents along a passage such as the digestive tract.

Items: p007-i8, p007-i9

Outside explanation: Extracellular matrix means material outside cells and provides a useful framework for comparing the very different appearances of connective tissues.

### Compact bone: Haversian canals and canaliculi · structure

Book basis: Compact bone is drawn as circular units with concentric rings around central Haversian canals and fine channels extending through the surrounding bone. A Haversian canal contains blood vessels and nerve fibres, whereas a canaliculus contains a slender process of a bone cell, or osteocyte. Distinguish the central canal from the fine channel by both position and contents; the nearby red blood corpuscle is a separately identified blood cell.

Book basis: Areolar tissue is illustrated as a loose fibre network containing several cell types, with fine branching reticular fibres and thicker collagen fibres. Separate fibre labels from cell labels during identification: the network and its resident cells are different structural components.

### Neural tissue and the parts of successive neurons · structure

Book basis: Neural tissue responds to stimuli, and the signalling diagram identifies each neuron's cell body, a nucleus within it, branching dendrites, a long axon and terminal axon tips. An incoming axon belongs to a previous neuron, while the lower-left neuron's axon extends rightwards and upwards towards the dendrites of the next neuron, whose cell body and nucleus are also labelled. Outside explanation: In this pathway, dendrites receive incoming information, the cell body contains the nucleus, and the axon carries the impulse towards its terminal branches; the nucleus is not a step in the impulse's route.

### Impulse direction and communication across synapses · process

Book basis: The open-ended question asks, 'How do neurons transmit impulses (send message to other cells)?', and the diagram answers the visible route through successive neurons: an incoming axon contacts a receiving dendritic region at the lower-left synapse, and blue electrical-signal arrows run along that neuron's axon towards axon tips contacting the next neuron's dendrites at the upper synapse. Outside explanation: In a typical chemical synapse, arrival of an electrical impulse at an axon terminal leads to neurotransmitter release across a small gap; the transmitter acts on the receiving cell's membrane and can initiate a new electrical signal. Book basis: Blue arrows show signal direction, whereas black pointer lines identify parts; the supplied question has no options or printed answer.

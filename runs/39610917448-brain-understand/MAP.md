## Concepts

### Four basic types of animal tissue · classification

(Book) Our body has mainly four tissue types, each defined by one job: epithelial tissue provides covering or lining; muscular tissue helps in movement; neural tissue responds to stimuli; connective tissue supports, links and cushions. (Teaching tip) Anchor the chapter as 'cover, move, respond, support'. Every later detail is a sub-type or feature of one of these four.

### General features of epithelial tissue · structure

(Book) Epithelium covers most organs and cavities within the body and forms a barrier that keeps different body systems separate. Its cells are tightly packed into a continuous sheet, with only a small amount of cementing material and almost no intercellular spaces. All epithelium is usually separated from the underlying tissue by an extracellular fibrous basement membrane. (Outside) Close packing and cell junctions make epithelium a selective barrier that restricts uncontrolled passage between cells. How leaky it is varies between epithelia, so it is not a complete seal. Epithelium usually has no blood vessels of its own and is nourished by diffusion from the tissue beneath the basement membrane.

### Glandular epithelium · definition

(Book) Epithelial cells often acquire additional specialisation as gland cells, which secrete substances at the epithelial surface. Sometimes a portion of the epithelial tissue folds inward and a multicellular gland is formed; this is glandular epithelium. (Outside) A mucus-secreting goblet cell is a unicellular gland. Sweat and salivary glands are multicellular glands formed by infolding: their secretory cells are glandular epithelium, while their ducts are lined by other epithelia (see C06).

### Shapes of epithelial cells (Fig. 6.9) · structure

(Book, Fig. 6.9) The figure shows four epithelia: (a) squamous, a single thin layer of flat cells with irregular boundaries; (b) stratified squamous, a thick block of many layers with flatter cells at the top; (c) cuboidal, a single layer of cube-like cells with round nuclei; (d) columnar (ciliated), a single layer of tall cells with hair-like cilia on the free surface. Each sheet rests on an unlabelled blue base. The figure does not name it; it probably represents the basement membrane of C02 or the underlying tissue. (Outside) Naming rule: combine shape (squamous = flat; cuboidal = roughly as tall as wide; columnar = clearly taller than wide) with layering (simple = one layer; stratified = many layers; pseudostratified = a single layer that only looks layered, see C05).

### Simple epithelia: squamous, cuboidal, columnar · comparison

(Book) Simple squamous is a single layer of flat cells with irregular boundaries. It lines blood vessels, capillaries, the air sacs of the lungs and Bowman's capsule of the kidney, and serves transport by diffusion where minimal protection is required. Simple cuboidal is a single layer of 'short cylindrical cells' lining kidney tubules and glandular ducts; it may have microvilli, as in the proximal convoluted tubule, and does absorption and secretion. Simple columnar is a single layer of tall, slender cells, 'often ciliated', lining the digestive tract and upper respiratory tract; its functions are protection, absorption, mucus secretion and movement in a specific direction. (Outside, why) A thin single layer gives a short diffusion distance for gas exchange and filtration. Cuboidal cells are roughly as tall as wide, shorter than columnar cells. Microvilli are non-motile finger-like folds that increase surface area for absorption; motile cilia beat to move mucus or other material in one direction. (Outside, reading the columnar row correctly) The book groups digestive and respiratory linings in one simplified row, so do not read it as saying intestinal absorptive epithelium is ciliated. The lining of the stomach and intestine is simple columnar: intestinal absorptive cells bear microvilli (brush border), not cilia, and goblet cells among them secrete mucus. Ciliated simple columnar occurs in places such as the uterine (fallopian) tube. Much of the conducting respiratory tract (nasal cavity, trachea, bronchi) is lined by pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium. Pseudostratified means it looks multilayered because the nuclei lie at different heights, but it is really one layer: every cell touches the basement membrane, while not every cell reaches the free surface. Its cilia and goblet-cell mucus trap dust and sweep it upward, which is the 'movement in a specific direction' the book mentions.

### Stratified epithelia: squamous, cuboidal, columnar · comparison

(Book) Stratified squamous lines the mouth and vagina. It has several layers that are continuously sloughed off and regenerated: the lower layer is columnar and metabolically active, and older cells are pushed upward and become flat. Its function is protection. Stratified cuboidal is found in mammary, sweat and salivary glands. Its upper layer is cuboid and the other layers may be cuboidal or other types; it protects the ducts of various glands. Stratified columnar is found in the male urethra and the lobar ducts of salivary glands. It has a layer of columnar cells on squamous, columnar or cuboidal cells, and does protection and secretion. (Outside) A stratified epithelium is named by the shape of its top (superficial) layer, which is why its deeper cells may differ. Many layers plus constant renewal let it withstand wear, such as friction from food in the mouth. Unlike pseudostratified epithelium, its upper cells do not touch the basement membrane.

### Skeletal muscle · fact

(Book) Location: skeleton. Function: movement, heat, posture. Appearance: striated, multinucleated with eccentric nuclei, fibres parallel. Control: voluntary. (Outside) Skeletal muscles attach to bones and move them at joints. Shivering is rapid skeletal muscle contraction that produces heat. Each fibre forms by the fusion of many cells, hence its many nuclei.

Items: p007-i3

### Cardiac muscle · fact

(Book) Location: heart. Function: pump blood continuously. Appearance: striated, one central nucleus. Control: involuntary. The table drawing (p007-i2) shows branched, interlinked cells. (Outside) Gap junctions at the intercalated discs couple the cells electrically and help coordinate contraction; the atria and ventricles contract in an organised sequence. (Exam tip) Cardiac muscle is the classic odd one out: striated yet involuntary.

Items: p007-i4

### Visceral (smooth) muscle · fact

(Book) Location: G.I. tract, uterus, eye, blood vessels. Function: peristalsis, blood pressure, pupil size, erection of hairs. Appearance: no striations, one central nucleus (a spindle-shaped cell in the drawing). Control: involuntary. (Outside) Smooth muscle in vessel walls narrows or widens the vessels and so changes blood pressure. Iris muscles change pupil size. The arrector pili muscles raise hairs, causing goosebumps. Uterine smooth muscle contracts during childbirth.

### Connective tissues – overview of Fig. 6.10 · classification

(Book) Connective tissue supports, links and cushions. Fig. 6.10 shows (a) types of blood cells, (b) compact bone, (c) hyaline cartilage, (d) areolar tissue and (e) adipose tissue. (Outside) Unlike epithelium, connective tissue cells usually lie scattered in an extracellular matrix of ground substance and fibres. The matrix may be fluid (plasma in blood), hard (bone), firm but flexible (cartilage) or loose (areolar).

### Blood tissue and its formed elements (Fig. 6.10a) · structure

(Book) Panel (a) shows several blood cells. Neutrophil (polymorphonuclear leucocyte), eosinophil and basophil are drawn with granular cytoplasm and a dark-stained nucleus. Lymphocyte and monocyte are the other white blood corpuscles. Platelets appear as small fragments, and the red blood corpuscle as a pale round disc. (Outside) Blood is the tissue itself: a fluid connective tissue in which RBCs, WBCs and platelets (the formed elements) float in plasma. Neutrophils, eosinophils and basophils are granulocytes; lymphocytes and monocytes are agranulocytes. WBCs defend against infection, platelets (cell fragments) help clotting, and RBCs carry oxygen using haemoglobin. Mature mammalian RBCs lack a nucleus and are typically biconcave discs. The shape is maintained by the membrane and its underlying cytoskeleton.

### Compact bone (Fig. 6.10b) · structure

(Book) Compact bone shows concentric rings around a Haversian canal, which contains blood vessels and nerve fibres. Canaliculi radiate from the bone cells and contain the slender processes of osteocytes. (Outside) Because the matrix is hard and calcified, nutrients and wastes move through the Haversian canal and the canalicular network. Each ring system is a Haversian system (osteon).

### Hyaline cartilage (Fig. 6.10c) · structure

(Book) Chondrocytes, often in pairs, lie in a clear hyaline matrix. (Outside) The matrix is firm yet flexible. Hyaline cartilage occurs at the ends of long bones and in the nose, the tracheal rings and the larynx. It has no blood vessels, so it heals slowly.

Items: p007-i12

### Areolar tissue (Fig. 6.10d) · structure

(Book) Areolar tissue shows thin branching reticular fibres and thick wavy collagen fibres. Its cells are a large star-shaped fibroblast, an irregular macrophage, a rounded mast cell and a plasma cell. (Outside) Fibroblasts make the fibres. Macrophages engulf microbes and debris; circulating monocytes can enter tissues and become macrophages, especially during inflammation, but many tissue-resident macrophages arise from embryonic precursors and maintain themselves locally. Mast cells release histamine. Plasma cells, derived from B lymphocytes, make antibodies. Areolar tissue is a loose connective tissue beneath the skin and around organs, where it packs and defends.

### Adipose tissue (Fig. 6.10e) · structure

(Book) Adipose tissue consists of adipocytes. Each cell is filled by a large fat droplet, with a small nucleus pressed to the cell edge. (Outside) It stores energy as fat, insulates the body under the skin and cushions organs such as the kidneys.

### Structure of a neuron · structure

(Book, diagram) Each depicted neuron has a star-shaped cell body with a dark nucleus, short branched dendrites, one long axon and branched axon tips. The first neuron's dendrites receive the axon of the previous neuron. (Outside) In general, a neuron has a cell body (soma) containing the nucleus, dendrites that usually receive signals, and an axon that carries signals away to its terminals. The shape of the cell body varies between neurons. Neurons are the signalling cells of neural tissue, which the book says responds to stimuli.

Items: p007-i17, p007-i19, p007-i20, p007-i21, p007-i22

### Impulse transmission and the synapse · process

(Book, diagram) The signal path is: axon of the previous neuron → synapse → dendrites → cell body → along the axon as an electrical signal → axon tips → synapse → dendrites of the next neuron. A synapse is the junction between the axon tips of one neuron and the dendrites of the next. (Outside) At the usual chemical synapse, the presynaptic terminal releases a neurotransmitter, which acts on receptors of the postsynaptic cell and can trigger a new electrical response. Transmission is typically one-way because release happens on the presynaptic side and the receptors sit on the postsynaptic side. Electrical synapses also exist but are not shown here.

- **p007-i18** · diagram_label · definition · image · exam high
  - synapse – red circles marking the junction between the axon (tips) of one neuron and the dendrites of the next
    - what: What is a synapse? → The junction where one neuron passes its message to the next neuron, as marked by the red circles.
      - what: What are the parts of a chemical synapse? → (1) The presynaptic terminal: the axon tip of the sending neuron, containing vesicles of neurotransmitter. (2) The synaptic cleft: the very narrow gap between the two cells. (3) The postsynaptic membrane: the receiving neuron's membrane, e.g. on a dendrite, carrying receptors. The synapse is the whole junction; the cleft is only the gap within it. _(outside the book)_
      - how: How is the message carried across if the cells do not touch? → At a chemical synapse, the presynaptic terminal releases neurotransmitters into the cleft. They diffuse across and bind receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, triggering an electrical change in the next neuron. At the less common electrical synapses, gap junctions connect the cells directly. _(outside the book)_
